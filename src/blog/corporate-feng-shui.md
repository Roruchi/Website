> Editorial status: idea brief. Start with one article before committing to a full series.

## Working promise

Use feng shui as a provocative metaphor, not as a mystical claim. The foundation should be environmental psychology, visual hierarchy, cognitive bias, and leadership presence.

The central idea: the room is already communicating before the meeting starts.

## Possible hook

Every room has an invisible org chart.

Who sits where, what receives visual emphasis, which words remain on the wall, and where attention moves all influence how safe, important, or welcome people feel.

## Four lenses

### 1. Position

How seating, distance, sight lines, movement, and access to exits affect participation and perceived authority.

### 2. Color

How contrast, visual weight, and environmental cues affect energy, focus, urgency, and calm.

### 3. Words

How labels, agendas, posters, dashboards, and repeated phrases tell people what the organization actually values.

### 4. Attention

How leaders distribute eye contact, questions, silence, and follow-up. Attention is the strongest signal in the room.

## First article shape

1. Open with a recognizable meeting-room scene.
2. Introduce practical feng shui as a design lens.
3. Explain why space and signals affect behavior.
4. Apply the four lenses to one ordinary workplace.
5. End with one core principle, one practical cheat sheet, and one golden rule.

## Boundaries

- Write for skeptical technology and leadership readers.
- Do not present metaphor as science.
- Source psychological claims before publication.
- Keep the North Star subtle: clarity, trust, and human potential.
- Avoid turning the piece into generic office-design advice.
- Visual posters are optional. The article must work without them.
