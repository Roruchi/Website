A while ago, I was preparing for a difficult conversation. We already had different opinions, and I expected those differences to surface as soon as we sat down.

I could have booked any meeting room, taken a seat across the table, and started explaining my point of view. Instead, I made two small choices. I found a calm room, away from distractions, and sat next to the other person rather than opposite them.

We still had different opinions. But the conversation gave us room to understand where the other was coming from. By the end of the meeting, we understood each other better.

I kept wondering: **what would have happened if we had sat opposite each other?**

Maybe nothing would have changed. We might have had exactly the same conversation. But I couldn't shake the thought that sitting side by side had helped us look at the situation together, rather than at each other across a dividing line.

![Two top-down meeting-room layouts compare facing each other across a table with sitting side by side toward a shared point of attention.](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-01-conversation.svg)

*The room and the disagreement can stay the same. The way we orient ourselves to each other can change. [View full-size diagram](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-01-conversation.svg).*

We spend time preparing what we want to say. We rehearse arguments, collect evidence, and imagine difficult questions. But how often do we prepare the **space in which the conversation happens**?

Every room has an invisible org chart. The person at the head of the table, the colleague with their back to the door, the screen everyone faces, the chair no one chooses: each gives us clues about the way the room is meant to be used.

The interesting part is that we usually notice those clues only when something feels wrong. The meeting room is too loud. We cannot see someone's face. The light hurts our eyes. People keep walking behind us. The room may be technically adequate, yet we would rather be anywhere else.

That is why I started looking more closely at feng shui.

## Why pay attention to the room?

**Where it comes from.** Feng shui is not a modern office-design trend. It is a Chinese tradition concerned with the relationship between people, their surroundings, and the movement of *qi*, often translated as vital energy. It has developed over centuries, through different schools and interpretations, drawing on ideas about landscapes, shelter, orientation, balance, yin and yang, and the five phases or elements. A clear historical introduction is Ole Bruun's [*An Introduction to Feng Shui*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511800931.005).

In its traditional context, feng shui is more than arranging furniture for comfort. Its ideas about qi and harmony are part of a wider Chinese understanding of the world. I do not want to strip that away and pretend feng shui is simply environmental psychology with a more interesting name.

At the same time, I am interested in what happens when we bring its questions into an ordinary workplace. Where does movement enter the room? Which positions feel supported? Where does our attention go? Is the space balanced for the people using it?

Cliff Tan, the designer behind [Dear Modern](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/feng-shui-modern-9781526639998/), is especially good at making these questions tangible. He shows how the placement of a desk or bed changes our relationship with a door, a window, or the rest of a room. His approach makes feng shui something you can explore with a floor plan rather than just read about in abstract terms.

**Why should I care?** Because a room is never a neutral container. It offers some actions more readily than others. A quiet corner invites concentration. A central table invites gathering. A seat next to a busy doorway asks us to keep noticing movement. None of these effects is automatic, and different people will experience the same space differently. But the environment is part of what we bring into a conversation, whether we planned it or not.

This article is my attempt to make that more visible. It is not a claim that moving a chair guarantees better decisions. It is an invitation to experiment with the conditions in which those decisions are made.

## Position Is Power

### Where does it come from?

One familiar feng shui concept is the **command position**. In practical terms, it usually means placing yourself where you can see the entrance without sitting directly in its path, ideally with some sense of support behind you. Feng shui practitioner [Anjie Cho describes this arrangement](https://www.anjiecho.com/soothing-home-office-decor/) for a home office; Cliff Tan also discusses it in his [workplace advice published by Singapore's Public Service Division](https://www.psd.gov.sg/challenge/articles/improve-your-energy-at-work-with-practical-feng-shui/).

There is something immediately recognizable about it. Think of the seat in a café from which you can see who comes in, while nobody needs to squeeze past your back. Now think of a workstation beside a doorway where people appear unexpectedly over your shoulder. Both might be perfectly usable. They do not feel the same.

![Top-down meeting room with one seat offering an overview of the entrance, and another seat more exposed to the doorway and circulation.](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-02-command-position.svg)

*The command position is about awareness of the room and a sense of support, not an automatic right to lead. [View full-size diagram](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-02-command-position.svg).*

### Why should I care?

At work, position can become a signal of status even when no one assigns it that meaning. A person at the end of a long conference table may have an easier time directing attention; someone tucked into a corner may have to work harder to enter the discussion. In one [1976 experimental study](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872677602901104), a leader seated at the end of a rectangular table gained an advantage in directing a group compared with a leader at a circular table. That is a specific result from a small research context, not a universal law about furniture.

A seat can also affect how prepared we feel to participate. When I cannot comfortably see the room or the people in it, some of my attention goes into compensating. I lean forward, turn around, or wait for an opening I might otherwise have noticed.

Then there is the question of intent. A long table is useful when someone needs to present, chair a formal decision, or direct a discussion. A small table or a side-by-side arrangement may suit a conversation where the task is to understand a problem together. Neither arrangement is inherently superior. The question is what kind of interaction you are inviting.

### How can I apply it?

Before you choose a chair, take a moment to look at the room. Where is the entrance? Who has a view of the people speaking? Which seats invite people to talk with each other, and which seem to aim everyone's attention at one person?

For a difficult one-to-one conversation, consider a corner of a table or sitting next to one another with something you can both look at. For a meeting that needs a clear decision, a more directional setup might help. When facilitating, pay attention to whether your position makes it easier to listen to everyone or accidentally puts you at the centre of every exchange.

And don't confuse command with dominance. You can choose a stable, comfortable position while leaving space for other people to lead.

## The Flow of a Room

### Where does it come from?

In feng shui, **qi** is central to how a place is understood. Paths, openings, obstacles, and transitions are not merely practical details; they are part of the relationship between the environment and its inhabitants. Different feng shui traditions interpret this in different ways. Cliff Tan's [*Feng Shui Modern*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/feng-shui-modern-9781526639998/) invites readers to notice walking paths, walls, windows, and doors as they consider a room.

One of Tan's examples is the contrast between being tucked out of the way and sitting where everyone passes. In an [interview with Tatler Asia](https://www.tatlerasia.com/homes/architecture-design/feng-shui-design-tips-architect-cliff-tan-dear-modern-tiktok), he explains how a corridor can feel uncomfortable because of the constant activity passing through it.

You might call that movement, distraction, or energy. Whatever language you use, you have probably felt it.

### Why should I care?

Picture two desks in the same office. One sits just beside the main route to the coffee machine. People cross behind it, stop to say hello, and glance at the screen. The other is in a quieter area where passing traffic stays outside the immediate workspace.

The first might be wonderful for a colleague who thrives on spontaneous conversation. It might be exhausting for someone trying to finish a complex design or concentrate on a difficult problem. The second can support focus, yet might leave someone who relies on quick collaboration feeling isolated.

![Top-down workplace layout showing a protected focus area away from the main circulation route and an exposed workstation next to the route.](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-03-room-flow.svg)

*A busy path is not a bad thing. It becomes a problem when it runs straight through a place meant for concentration. [View full-size diagram](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-03-room-flow.svg).*

This is why I like to think of flow in terms of **what the room is asking people to do**. Some spaces ask us to move, meet, and exchange ideas. Others ask us to settle and stay with one thing. Trouble starts when we expect both from the same chair, at the same time.

There is research behind part of this intuition. A [2023 analysis of occupant surveys from more than 600 office buildings](https://doi.org/10.5334/bc.274) found acoustics, privacy, and space among the leading sources of dissatisfaction. That does not test qi, but it reinforces the practical importance of understanding how an office actually feels to the people working in it.

### How can I apply it?

Watch the room for five minutes before rearranging anything. Where do people naturally walk? Which areas invite interruptions? Does the route to the door run behind someone's chair? Is the discussion table close enough to the quieter workstations that every conversation becomes everyone's conversation?

If you can, move focused work away from the main walking path and keep circulation clear. Let collaboration happen where people can gather without interrupting others. In a small room, even shifting a desk, turning a chair, or moving a shelf can give people a better relationship with the space.

The goal is not to eliminate movement. A workplace without movement would be a rather strange workplace. The goal is to give movement somewhere useful to go.

## The Energy of a Space

### Where does it come from?

Ask someone how a room feels and you might hear: *calm*, *restless*, *heavy*, *inviting*, *draining*. We often reach for the word **energy** because it captures something we notice as a whole, before we have identified every detail.

In feng shui, qi has a deeper traditional meaning than this everyday use of the word. It would be misleading to treat the two as identical. But the language of energy gives me a useful question to bring into a room: **what in this space is supporting us, and what is asking something of us?**

Traditionally, feng shui also works with yin and yang and the five phases: wood, fire, earth, metal, and water. They carry relationships and symbolism within Chinese cosmology. I am not proposing a corporate checklist that requires one object from each category. What interests me is the invitation to look for balance rather than maximizing one quality everywhere.

There is a separate connection I find meaningful in Japanese Zen-influenced aesthetics. In a tea room, carefully chosen objects, natural materials, and an uncluttered setting create room to attend to an encounter. The [Metropolitan Museum of Art's account of the Japanese tea ceremony](https://www.metmuseum.org/ja/essays/the-japanese-tea-ceremony) describes this deliberate setting and its emphasis on the interaction between host, guests, and utensils. Zen and feng shui are distinct traditions with different histories. I bring them together here because both have something to teach me about paying attention to where I am.

### Why should I care?

Imagine starting a sensitive conversation in a room with harsh overhead lighting, a projector humming, the air feeling stale, and people talking just outside the door. Nothing dramatic is happening. Yet the room keeps demanding little pieces of attention.

Now imagine that the light is comfortable, the sound is manageable, you can hear each other clearly, and the table has enough free space to think. The problem you came to discuss is unchanged, but some of the effort of being there has disappeared.

![Two views of the same room contrast competing sensory demands with quieter light, sound, and a clearer point of focus.](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-04-sensory-balance.svg)

*The difference is not expensive furniture. It is how many things compete for attention at once. [View full-size diagram](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-04-sensory-balance.svg).*

We do have research on some of these ingredients, though not on a single universal formula for a room's energy. The [600-building study](https://doi.org/10.5334/bc.274) found problems with acoustics and speech privacy particularly common. A [2023 systematic review of nature in workplaces](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare11212838) found promising, but limited and varied, evidence for some nature-related approaches to reducing stress. A [2014 review of colour psychology](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115035) found that colour can affect experience and behaviour, while warning against broad claims that one colour always produces one response.

That last point matters. A blue wall does not magically make a team productive. A plant does not cancel a noisy room. Even a beautiful space can feel wrong if it asks us to work in a way that does not suit the task.

### How can I apply it?

Start with the senses rather than the furniture catalogue. Can people hear one another without raising their voices? Is the lighting comfortable at different times of day? Is there glare on the screen? Is the room too warm, too cold, or too full of competing signals?

Then look at visual balance. A wall of dashboards, posters, and status indicators can give information, but it can also make every surface compete for attention. A single useful whiteboard might do more for a discussion than five screens displaying metrics nobody needs in that moment.

Finally, consider nature and material. A view outside, a plant, daylight, or a natural texture may be pleasant and restorative for some people. Those are invitations to try, not substitutes for asking the people using the room how it feels.

I also try to remember something from Zen practice: creating space is not necessarily adding another calming object. Sometimes it means removing one distraction, allowing a pause, or leaving a surface empty.

## The Room Is Speaking

### Where does it come from?

The original feng shui question is about a person's relationship with their environment, not just where their furniture stands. An environment contains symbols as well as physical objects. In a modern office, some of the strongest symbols are words.

A sign saying **War Room** tells a different story from one saying **Project Room**. A dashboard displaying only incidents invites a different conversation from one showing both reliability and improvements. A meeting invitation titled **Approval Required** sets a different expectation from **Decision to Discuss**.

Those are not ancient feng shui rules. They are my extension of the same attention to surroundings: the things we put in a room help tell people what kind of place they have entered.

### Why should I care?

Imagine walking into a project room where a giant red number shows how far the team is behind schedule. It might be useful information. But if it is the only thing on the wall, the room may become a constant reminder of pressure. Now imagine the same team keeping that metric visible alongside its plan, progress, known risks, and the next decision it can take. The problem has not vanished; the room now offers a way to engage with it.

Words have a way of becoming part of the furniture. We stop seeing the poster that says *Own It*, the old architecture diagram no one has updated, or the agenda template that puts every conversation under *Risks and Escalations*. Newcomers still see them.

### How can I apply it?

At your next meeting, look at the room from the perspective of someone attending for the first time. What is written on the walls? What is visible on the screen before the meeting starts? What do the room name and agenda suggest people are expected to do?

Keep useful information visible. Take outdated messages down. Put the problem in the centre when you want people to work on the problem together. And if a phrase seems to create the wrong atmosphere, change the phrase rather than asking everyone to ignore it.

It is a small intervention, but sometimes the easiest way to change a room is to change what it keeps repeating.

## Presence Changes the Room

### Where does it come from?

Feng shui begins with placement, but we are not static pieces of furniture. We turn toward someone, lean away, move a chair, stand up, share a screen, or fall silent. Our relationship to the room keeps changing as we use it.

In an interview with [Tatler Asia](https://www.tatlerasia.com/homes/architecture-design/feng-shui-design-tips-architect-cliff-tan-dear-modern-tiktok), Cliff Tan puts a useful piece of this into words:

> "A lot of how we behave depends on how we potentially might feel."

I keep coming back to that thought. The arrangement of the space may influence how we feel, but we also shape the space through our presence within it.

### Why should I care?

Think about a workshop where everyone faces the facilitator, who is standing beside the screen. Even if the facilitator asks for collaboration, the room still says *look at me*. When the facilitator steps aside and lets people gather around a shared board, the physical centre of attention moves.

Neither setup is wrong. A presentation needs a focal speaker. A group trying to solve a problem may benefit from a shared object in the middle. The mismatch matters more than the layout itself.

![Two meeting-room diagrams contrast a single speaker-led focal point with a facilitator making space for a shared board.](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-05-attention.svg)

*Where attention gathers changes the role everyone is invited to play. [View full-size diagram](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-05-attention.svg).*

There is also a digital version. On a video call we can be boxed into an arrangement where every face appears to look at us. Research on videoconference fatigue, including work by [Jeremy Bailenson at Stanford](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2021/02/four-causes-zoom-fatigue-solutions), describes the potential burden of constant gaze and heightened nonverbal attention. We may be physically alone, but the screen creates a social space of its own.

### How can I apply it?

If you are facilitating, notice where you put yourself. Are you blocking the board? Are people talking to you when you wanted them to talk to each other? Does everyone have a reasonable line of sight to the person speaking?

Try moving to the side when the group should own the discussion. Give people time to think without filling every silence. In a remote meeting, consider whether the entire conversation needs faces on screen, whether the shared material can take centre stage, and whether people can comfortably look away without being judged.

This is where I find my Zen practice helpful. I do not have to occupy every silence. Sometimes being present means making room for someone else.

## One Meeting, Two Experiences

Let's bring the pieces together with an illustrative example. This is not a report of an actual customer workshop; it is a way to test the ideas against a familiar situation.

A team has an important disagreement about a technical direction. They book the first available room. The projector is running, even though there is no presentation yet. Someone takes the head of the table. Two people with different views end up sitting opposite each other. Half the group cannot see the whiteboard clearly. People pass behind the seats nearest the door.

The meeting can still go well. Good intentions, clear questions, and mutual respect count for far more than furniture. But the room is not doing much to help.

Now imagine making a few small changes before people arrive. The group moves into a quieter space. The whiteboard is visible from every seat. The two people with different ideas can sit at an angle and look toward a shared sketch. The facilitator sits where they can see everyone, but does not become the only person people address. The doorway stays clear. The screen is turned off until it is useful.

![A before-and-after floor plan of the same meeting contrasts divided attention and obstructed circulation with a shared board and clearer spatial arrangement.](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-06-meeting-redesign.svg)

*Same people, same disagreement, different invitations from the room. These are possible arrangements, not predicted outcomes. [View full-size diagram](/assets/images/corporate-feng-shui-06-meeting-redesign.svg).*

Nothing here guarantees agreement. It should not. The purpose of a good conversation is not to engineer consent. It is to help people think clearly, hear each other, and work through what matters.

That brings me back to the difficult conversation I described at the beginning. I cannot know what would have happened if we had sat across the table. But I am glad I considered the room before we began. Choosing a calm place and a position that felt collaborative was a small act of care for the conversation.

Sometimes that is what a practical application of feng shui looks like. Not a grand redesign. A thoughtful decision about where and how to sit.

## Your Five-Minute Feng Shui Check

You do not need to be the owner of a building, the person booking the meeting rooms, or an expert in feng shui to experiment with these ideas.

The next time you enter a workspace, give yourself five minutes to notice what is already happening:

1. **Position.** Can people see and hear each other? Who has an overview of the room? Does the seating match the purpose of the conversation?
2. **Flow.** Where do people enter and walk? Are there interruptions in the place where you need concentration? Is the path clear?
3. **Energy.** What do you notice first: light, noise, temperature, movement, clutter, or a sense of calm? What is one thing you could adjust?
4. **Signals.** What is the room communicating through its screens, signs, boards, and repeated words? Is that message still useful?
5. **Presence.** Where is everyone's attention going? Can you change your own position to make it easier for others to take part?

Choose **one** small change, not five. Move a chair. Turn a table. Open a blind. Find a quieter corner. Put a shared sketch where everyone can see it. Then ask people whether the change helped, and be prepared to change it back.

The principles are not a scoring system. A busy area can be exactly right for a social meeting; a commanding seat can be unnecessary in an intimate conversation; a quiet room can be too quiet for a lively workshop. Feng shui invites us to look at relationships between people and places, not chase a universal perfect layout.

I started with a question about two chairs facing each other. I ended up noticing something wider: we participate in our surroundings, and our surroundings participate in how we meet one another.

**Before your next important conversation, prepare what you want to say. Then take one more moment to prepare the space in which you will say it.**

## Sources and a Note on Evidence

I have drawn on traditional feng shui, the practical work of contemporary practitioners, Zen-influenced approaches to space, and studies of workplaces and behaviour. They are different kinds of sources and do not establish the same kinds of claims. The diagrams illustrate possible situations; they are not experimental results.

- **Traditional context:** Ole Bruun, [*An Introduction to Feng Shui*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511800931.005), Cambridge University Press (2008), chapter 5, on the schools and concepts of feng shui. Ke-Tsung Han and Jin-Kai Lin, [a systematic review of empirical feng shui studies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2023.e19532) (*Heliyon*, 2023), which found some promising results for environmental features while noting limitations in research quality and unresolved questions about effects on humans.
- **Contemporary practice:** Cliff Tan, [*Feng Shui Modern*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/feng-shui-modern-9781526639998/) (Bloomsbury, 2022); [Tan's advice on workspaces](https://www.psd.gov.sg/challenge/articles/improve-your-energy-at-work-with-practical-feng-shui/) published by Singapore's Public Service Division; [Tatler Asia's interview](https://www.tatlerasia.com/homes/architecture-design/feng-shui-design-tips-architect-cliff-tan-dear-modern-tiktok) with Tan; Anjie Cho, [*Soothing Home Office Decor*](https://www.anjiecho.com/soothing-home-office-decor/).
- **Seating and social interaction:** R. Lécuyer, [*Social Organization and Spatial Organization*](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872677602901104) (*Human Relations*, 1976). The findings concern particular group tasks and seating configurations, not every meeting.
- **Workplace conditions:** Thomas Parkinson and colleagues, [*Common sources of occupant dissatisfaction with workspace environments in 600 office buildings*](https://doi.org/10.5334/bc.274) (*Buildings & Cities*, 2023); [*Nature in the Office*](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare11212838) (*Healthcare*, 2023), a review of workplace nature interventions; Andrew J. Elliot and Markus A. Maier, [*Color Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115035) (*Annual Review of Psychology*, 2014).
- **Zen and tea-room aesthetics:** Anna Willmann, [*The Japanese Tea Ceremony*](https://www.metmuseum.org/ja/essays/the-japanese-tea-ceremony), The Metropolitan Museum of Art (2011). This is a parallel source of inspiration, not the historical origin of Chinese feng shui.
- **Online presence:** Jeremy Bailenson, [*Four causes for Zoom fatigue and their solutions*](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2021/02/four-causes-zoom-fatigue-solutions), Stanford Report (2021).

The personal opening describes my own experience. It does not establish that sitting side by side *caused* the better outcome. Feng shui's account of qi is presented as part of the tradition; modern studies of environmental conditions do not by themselves prove or disprove that account. The practical suggestions are invitations to observe and experiment, not universal psychological laws.
